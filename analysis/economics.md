# Economics

Flock protocol - DRAFT v0.3 - 2026-06-12

This document analyzes who pays what, who earns what, and where the model breaks. Constants are normative per [AMENDMENTS-v0.3](../research/AMENDMENTS-v0.3.md) and [ERRATA](../research/ERRATA.md) ([synthesis-v0.2](../research/raw/synthesis-v0.2.md) is the historical baseline; where they conflict, the amendments win). Mechanisms referenced here are specified in [specs/onchain.md](../specs/onchain.md) and [specs/event-kinds.md](../specs/event-kinds.md); the adversarial findings behind several cost bounds are in [attacks.md](attacks.md). All "sats @ N sat/vB" figures are arithmetic on vbyte counts, not market predictions.

## 1. Operator model

### 1.1 Revenue lines

| Line | Price | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Refresh fees | **0.5–0.9% of refreshed value per refresh**, priced on the ~58-day capital lock each refresh creates | Pass-through of cost of capital (break-even ≈0.8% per refresh at 5% APY, §1.2); near-zero margin |
| Arkoor cosign | 1–3 sats flat + 0.05% | Near-zero marginal cost; the margin line |
| Gateway swaps (vHTLC/PTLC ↔ BOLT11/12) | 0.1–0.5% of bridged volume | Volume business; competitive with LN service fees |
| Vault/relay pinning subscriptions | ~1–2k sats/month per user | Service business with real storage costs; faces competition from independent relays |

**Why per-refresh duration pricing is load-bearing.** Bark-style refresh pricing charges for the new leaf's *remaining lifetime*, which makes refreshing early cost more and pushes users toward late refresh ([second.tech](https://second.tech/docs/learn/intro)). But lifetime pricing creates a churn-amplification lever, identified as a funded-griefing attack in [attacks.md](attacks.md): every refresh forces Q to front a fresh tranche of capital that is recovered only at the old round's root sweep (~58 days after funding at the canonical `sweep_delay`, §1.2), so an unthrottled attacker who boards a balance and refreshes it hourly strands up to ~1,390 tranches of operator capital while paying fees on only one balance — **~1,390× theoretical capital leverage**, enough to drain the recycling float and halt honest refreshes. Two controls close it. Pricing each refresh on the **actual ~58-day lock it creates** makes every refresh pay for exactly the tranche it strands: hourly churn costs the attacker ≈0.8% of the balance *per refresh*, so the attack funds its own damage. And the **minimum dwell time `D` between refreshes** bounds the leverage any single balance can exert at **58 d / D** (e.g., D = 7 days ⇒ ≈8×), with per-leaf rate limits as backstop. [attacks.md](attacks.md) G4 uses the same figures.

A second consequence of per-refresh pricing cuts against users and is conceded plainly: refresh *frequency* is a real cost axis. A casual user refreshing once per deadline-driven ~23-day cycle pays 0.5–0.9% per cycle; a merchant whose VaR-cap crossings or hop-4 resets force frequent checkpoints pays the same percentage *per event* and therefore proportionally more (§2.2). Merchants at hop-4 velocity will prefer LN-gateway sweeps or on-chain offboards to high-frequency refreshes — an extension of open problem #7. `sweep_delay` and pool expiry are the pricing levers: shorter `sweep_delay` means cheaper refreshes but a smaller dormancy grace window (§1.2, §5).

### 1.2 Cost lines

1. **Cost of capital on the recycling float — the dominant cost.** Boarding-funded leaves cost Q ≈ nothing: the user's own UTXO funds the cell input. Q's float comes from **refresh churn**. At each refresh Q fronts the full value of the new leaf and recovers the matching capital only at the old round's **root sweep**: forfeited leaves become spendable by Q at `T_exp + sweep_delay`, not at expiry ([spark.money](https://www.spark.money/research/ark-protocol-explained), [docs.arklabs.xyz/ark.pdf](https://docs.arklabs.xyz/ark.pdf)). Each refresh therefore creates a tranche locked for `(4320 + sweep_delay)/144` days — **~58 days** at the canonical `sweep_delay = 4032`, not the ~30-day expiry window v0.2 modeled. The float scales with refresh cadence — **float ≈ hot_balance × 58 d / refresh_cycle**:

   | Refresh cycle | What sets it | Float ÷ hot balance | User cost/month @0.8%/refresh |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | 30 d | full expiry — unreachable; the deadline and receive ban bind first | ≈1.9× | ≈0.8% |
   | **23 d** | wallet refresh deadline `T_exp − 1008` — the canonical hot cycle | ≈2.5× | ≈1.0% |
   | 16 d | receive ban (no arkoor receives < 2016 blocks to `T_exp`) | ≈3.6× | ≈1.5% |
   | 2 d | high-velocity hop-4 / VaR checkpointing | ≈29× | ≈12% |

   Break-even refresh fee ≈ APY × (4320 + sweep_delay)/52560 ≈ **0.8% per refresh** at 5% APY — each refresh must pay for the ~58-day lock it creates, at any cadence. The posted band is **0.5–0.9% per refresh**: break-even (≈0.8%) sits near the top of the band, so 0.9% roughly clears cost of capital and anything below ~0.8% is a loss-leader cross-subsidized by the cosign/gateway/pinning lines (§1.3). Newly-dormant balances add a small float tail — the final tranche stays locked ~58 days after the last refresh, then leaves the float for good. Structural trade-off: a pool can cheapen refreshes by shortening `sweep_delay` (2016 → ~44-day lock, break-even ≈0.6% per refresh) — but the dormancy grace window is `sweep_delay − 288` blocks, so cheaper refreshes mean a shorter window for towers to exit dormant funds before the sweep (§2.3, §5).
2. **On-chain cell-tx fees.** Sixteen cell txs per pool per hour (each: one cell root + up to 64 per-user connectors + Q change; CPFP-only fee-bumping — RBF would void the pre-signed graph). Size scales with the number of refreshing users that hour; the fee budget is recovered inside refresh fees. [TBD: canonical cell-tx vbyte budget — depends on connector count per cell.]
3. **Dedicated fee UTXOs.** CPFP reserves that cannot be conflicted, required by the pinning defenses (TRUC/P2A, pre-signed min-fee children). Working capital plus fees actually burned during exit incidents and forfeit races.
4. **Infrastructure.** Seven independent organizations each running signing infrastructure behind hidden services, relay capacity, and ciphertext archives. Materially trivial next to the float — thousands-of-dollars-per-month class, not BTC class.

### 1.3 Worked example: the 12.6 BTC float

This is the **single canonical worked example** for Flock economics; whitepaper §9 quotes it verbatim, and any other variant must be labeled "optimistic, full-adoption". A pool of 10,000 users at 100,000 sats average balance custodies ~10 BTC (10⁹ sats). Half is hot — 5 BTC actively refreshed on the deadline-driven 23-day cycle (§1.2). Float ≈ 5 × 58/23 ≈ **12.6 BTC** (plus a small newly-dormant tail); cost of capital ≈ 12.6 × 5%/12 ≈ **0.052 BTC/month**. Refresh revenue at 0.8% per refresh ≈ 5 × 0.8% × 30/23 ≈ **0.052 BTC/month** — the refresh line is sold ≈ at cost. The stack, with stated assumptions:

| Item | Assumption | Sats/month |
|---|---|---|
| Refresh revenue | 5 BTC hot × 0.8% per refresh (≈break-even, near top of the 0.5–0.9% band) × 30/23 cycles/mo | +5.2M |
| Cost of capital | 5% APY on ≈12.6 BTC float (5 × 58/23) | −5.2M |
| Arkoor cosign | 20 sends/user/mo, 20k sats avg → 2 + 10 sats each | +2.4M |
| Gateway swaps | volume-dependent | + (unmodeled) |
| Pinning subscriptions | ~30% adoption × 1–2k sats/user/mo, gross of real storage costs | +3–6M |
| Cell-tx fees + fee UTXOs + infra | see §1.2 | −0.5M to −1M (rough) |

Full-stack reconciliation: the refresh line approximately cancels cost of capital (+5.2M vs −5.2M — sold at cost); the margin is the arkoor cosign line (+2.4M) plus pinning at ~30% adoption (+3–6M gross, against real storage costs) minus overhead (−0.5–1M) ≈ **+4–7M sats/month, split seven ways** (before gateway volume, gross of pinning storage) — thin but positive. Pricing refreshes below ~0.8% is rational only as a loss-leader against gateway/pinning volume. **Honest framing: this is a capital-intensive, LSP-like business whose headline product is sold ≈ at cost at 10k users.** It concentrates among well-funded operators; the federation distributes *trust* across 7 organizations, it does not eliminate the capital-scale pressure. The repricing history is two corrections, not one: the v0.2 review ([review-v0.2-findings](../research/raw/review-v0.2-findings.json)) showed that once the true 58-day lock was modeled, v0.2's 0.3–0.4%/mo headline fee sat at or below cost of capital; the v0.3 re-verification ([review-v0.3-findings](../research/raw/review-v0.3-findings.json)) then showed that "%/month" itself understated active users' cost because the refresh deadline and receive ban force sub-monthly cycles — so v0.3 prices **per refresh** and lets cadence carry the cost.

### 1.4 The LSP migration path

Today's Lightning LSPs already run this balance sheet, worse: capital is pre-allocated *per channel edge*, every received payment depletes the inbound it used, and rebalancing is a continuous paid task ([lightning.engineering](https://docs.lightning.engineering/the-lightning-network/liquidity/how-to-get-inbound-capacity-on-the-lightning-network), [plaitr.com](https://www.plaitr.com/blog/lightning-network-for-merchant-payments-a-2026-reality-check)). The top-10 LN operators held ~62% of public capacity by Aug 2025 ([news.bitcoin.com](https://news.bitcoin.com/data-shows-sustained-slide-in-lightning-network-capacity-channels-through-2025/)) — the capital concentration already happened. A migrating LSP brings the same capital into a Flock quorum seat with better capital *shape*: one pooled float serves every user (arkoor payments consume **zero** operator liquidity at send time), there is nothing to rebalance, and no per-edge lockup — though the pooled float itself runs ≈2.5× hot balances at the deadline-driven 23-day cycle (§1.2). The gateway-swap line is the natural continuation of their existing LN routing business.

## 2. User costs by persona

### 2.1 Payer (hot balance)

- **Refresh cost**: 0.5–0.9% of the refreshed balance **per refresh**. A casual payer on the deadline-driven ~23-day cycle (§1.2) pays once per cycle: a 100k-sat spending balance costs 500–900 sats per refresh ≈ 650–1,170 sats/month (~8–14k sats/year).
- **Send fees**: 1–3 sats + 0.05% per payment.
- **Intent-bond exposure (small, recurring)**: every refresh intent is bonded for 1,000 sats (`intent_bond_sats`). The instrument is a **ledger debit, netted at next service**: on a post-nonce abort Q MAY record the slash in the transfer log and deduct it from the user's next successful refresh output or arkoor change, refusing service until netted. The backstop is a pre-signed **lien tx** on the refreshing leaf's cooperative path — (value − bond) back to the owner, bond to Q — valid on any future unroll of that leaf (collecting there costs Q ≈ an unroll; netting is the economic path). An honest user normally loses nothing, but the slash is Q's unilateral call — budget it as a recurring exposure of ≤1,000 sats per refresh intent, not a fee.
- **Watchtower height-deposit (contingent, not per-send)**: the deposit package carries pre-signed **escalating bump children (10 / 50 / 200 sat/vB) spending the owner's dedicated fee UTXOs**, each variant carrying a ~2,000-sat fee output (canonical) to whichever tower completes the broadcast; the tower selects the lowest variant that clears, so its own out-of-pocket cost is ≈0. At most one tower fee is ever paid, and **nothing is paid if no tower ever needs to act** — the deposit budget is a contingent allocation inside pre-signed children, not a payment in the send flow. Residual: feerates above the 200 sat/vB ceiling stall the package (folds into the replacement-cycling/fee-spike open problem, §4). The symmetry must be conceded: Flock criticizes Lightning watchtowers for lacking a fee model, then makes its own tower economy load-bearing (open problem #10) — the difference is that Flock's deposits carry an explicit, pre-funded escalation path.
- Worked month: 100k-sat hot balance refreshed once per ~23-day cycle, 20 sends of 20k sats → ≈650–1,170 sats refresh + ~240 sats send fees ≈ **890–1,410 sats/month, ~0.2–0.35% of turnover**, plus the contingent allocations above only if Q slashes or a tower acts.

What this replaces on Lightning: two on-chain transactions per channel (re-incurred on every reopen), inbound liquidity that is someone else's locked capital and must be bought or earned ([lightning.engineering](https://docs.lightning.engineering/the-lightning-network/liquidity/how-to-get-inbound-capacity-on-the-lightning-network)), continuous paid rebalancing ([plaitr.com](https://www.plaitr.com/blog/lightning-network-for-merchant-payments-a-2026-reality-check)), and a force-close tail risk that can exceed 1M sats at 50 sat/vB with full HTLC slots ([voltage.cloud](https://voltage.cloud/blog/lightning-node-management-handling-high-on-chain-fees)) — at that same 50 sat/vB, Flock's worst case is a ~80k-sat first exit and ~15.5k marginal (§3) — plus recovery that depends on a peer being honest, reachable, and running ([lnd safety.md](https://github.com/lightningnetwork/lnd/blob/master/docs/safety.md)). Flock's per-refresh cost is the explicit, predictable price of what Lightning charges implicitly and unpredictably. The honest scope at the repriced band: Flock is competitive with Lightning's channel open/close/rebalance amortization for **active payment balances**; it is *not* cheaper than a well-managed routing node's amortized costs, and at ~0.8% per ~23-day refresh (≈1%/month hot) it is a poor place to park a large long-held balance — which is why savings go dormant (§2.3) instead of staying hot.

### 2.2 Merchant / receiver

- **Receiving is free**: no inbound liquidity, no channel, no on-chain setup, no liveness; the sender pays the cosign fee and lodges the height-deposit (whose tower fee is contingent, §2.1).
- **Auto-checkpointing above the VaR cap (default 50,000 sats, applied to the aggregate uncheckpointed balance)**: any accepted receipt pushing the aggregate over the cap triggers auto-checkpoint at the **next available round seat** (VaR-triggered refreshes take priority in Q's seat assignment; structuring many sub-cap receipts does not evade the cap). Under per-refresh pricing this is where merchant cost concentrates: each auto-checkpoint is a refresh and pays 0.5–0.9% *of the checkpointed balance, per event*, plus the 1–3-sat cosign minimum.
- Worked month: 30M sats received. Checkpointing everything is the expensive path: pushing ~30M sats through VaR auto-checkpoints at 0.8% per refresh costs ~240k sats — **~0.8% of receipts**. The economic path for a high-velocity merchant is to ride the uncheckpointed tier up to the cap and **sweep out through an LN gateway (0.1–0.5% → 30–150k sats, ~0.1–0.5% of receipts)** or take periodic on-chain offboards (~3.1k sats per marginal exit, §3). Stated honestly (extending open problem #7): hop-4-velocity refreshing costs ~0.8% of flow; gateways and offboards are the designed relief valve. The comparable Lightning merchant must keep purchased inbound capacity ahead of expected receipts at all times ([plaitr.com](https://www.plaitr.com/blog/lightning-network-for-merchant-payments-a-2026-reality-check)).
- Balances left dormant stop paying refresh fees and are auto-exited on-chain ~32 days after the last checkpoint at the owner's expense (§2.3); balances offboarded on-chain pay exit costs once (§3).

**Velocity bound and checkpoint capacity.** Arkoor chains cap at 4 hops between refreshes: a hop-4 recipient — or a sender whose change reaches hop 4 — cannot transact again until the next hourly round checkpoints the leaf. So active users *do* wait for rounds, routinely; "payments never wait for a round" holds only below the velocity bound. Round checkpoint capacity is 16 cells × 64 leaves = **1,024 leaves/hour/pool**. For the 10k-user pool of §1.3 at 20 sends/user/month: ~278 sends/hour pool-wide; even if every send forced a checkpoint (VaR crossing or hop-4 reset), demand is ~278 + ~9/hour of baseline 23-day-cycle refreshes ≈ 28% of capacity — roughly 3.5× headroom. The failure mode is synchronized demand (a viral inflow pushing thousands of balances over the VaR cap in the same hour): excess auto-checkpoints queue for the next available seats — VaR-triggered refreshes take priority in Q's seat assignment — and the queued balances ride uncheckpointed, collusion-bounded, not lost, until their turn. Queue congestion proportionally extends the above-cap exposure window: capacity is a latency-and-exposure cost under burst, not a safety bound.

### 2.3 Saver

**No carrying fee — and one automatic on-chain exit, not an unconditional zero.** A saver stops refreshing and pays no further fees: the leaf rides to `T_exp`, the recovery tapleaf matures at `T_exp + 288`, and **~32 days after the last checkpoint** the funds are automatically exited to plain self-custody under the seed-derived cold key — at the owner's on-chain expense. What makes that exit happen is mechanical, not assumed: at **every checkpoint** the wallet pre-signs its recovery claim and lodges {unroll branch + recovery claim + escalating bump children (10/50/200 sat/vB) spending its dedicated fee UTXOs} as a self-funded kind-38404 height-deposit with `trigger_height = T_exp + 288` (also conflict-triggered), replicated to ≥3 towers. Dormancy is therefore **tower-protected self-custody with a scheduled landing**: dormant funds are never confiscated while towers function and the owner need never come online — but the funds do not stay in the pool, and the exit costs ≈3.1k sats (marginal) to ~16k sats (first exiter) at 10 sat/vB, 5× at 50, plus at most one contingent ~2,000-sat tower fee. The floor consequence is real: a leaf near the 10,000-sat minimum is substantially consumed by its own exit; savings-profile pools can stretch expiry and `sweep_delay` to lengthen the dormancy horizon. Absolute worst case — the owner stays absent AND no tower acts within the 3,744-block (~26-day) window — the quorum sweep at `T_exp + 4032` confiscates. The cell root carries a sweep leaf, so Q sweeps a never-unrolled cell at its root: v0.2's "Q must unroll to sweep" was false, and the dormancy guarantee is exactly as strong as the tower market behind it (open problem #10).

The lineage is worth stating plainly. The v0.1 baseline force-exited idle wallets on-chain after ~25 days at the user's expense (4.5k–22k sats) — recorded in the v0.1 review artifacts ([research/raw/](../research/raw/)). v0.2 grafted the recovery tapleaf and oversold the result as unconditional zero-cost dormancy; the v0.2 adversarial review ([review-v0.2-findings](../research/raw/review-v0.2-findings.json)) caught the sweep-vs-recovery race. v0.3 keeps an automatic exit — that is what the standing deposit *is* — and prices it: ~32 days after the last checkpoint, dormant funds land on-chain at the owner's expense (up to first-exiter scale, ~1.6 kvB, if the cell is otherwise untouched — §3, plus at most one contingent ~2,000-sat tower fee). What changed since v0.1 is the destination and the guarantee, not the existence of the exit: the funds land under the owner's own cold key with ≥3-tower redundancy, so the honest claim is "no carrying fee, never confiscated while towers function" — never "no forced exit". Re-entry costs a boarding, and the structural 0.5–0.9%-per-refresh cost still applies to anything kept *hot* — Flock is competitive for payment balances, not as a yield-free savings wrapper, which is why savings go dormant instead.

## 3. Exit economics

Per-transaction arithmetic (script-path inputs ≈ 126 vB; node tx ≈ 321 vB; claim tx ≈ 193 vB; TRUC zero-fee parents with pre-signed CPFP children):

| Scenario | vbytes | @2 sat/vB | @10 sat/vB | @50 sat/vB |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First exiter (depth-3 unroll: 3×321 + 193 + ~460 CPFP) | ≈1.6 kvB | ~3.2k sats | **~16k sats** | ~80k sats |
| Marginal exiter (path already unrolled, incl. own CPFP child) | ≈310 vB | ~620 sats | **~3.1k sats** | ~15.5k sats |
| Each arkoor hop in the exit chain (≤4 hops) | +235 vB | +470 sats | +2,350 sats | +11.8k sats |

Add latency, not fees: the claim matures per its ladder rung — 720 blocks for a checkpointed owner, down to 144 at hop 4 — after the leaf is on-chain.

**When exit is rational.** Exit when expected loss from staying exceeds exit cost: quorum-death heartbeat alarms (kind 31401), refresh censorship approaching the `T_exp − 1008` wallet deadline, or a fee-rate oracle predicting worse rates later. Early exiters subsidize later ones on shared branches ([second.tech](https://second.tech/docs/learn/exit)) — the first exiter pays ~5× the marginal rate — so rational users wait unless deadline-bound, and the system's defaults (towers exiting by `T_exp − 720`, hourly expiry laddering) are designed so almost all exits are marginal-cost exits.

**The dust floor and what lives below it.** The minimum leaf denomination is 10,000 sats — calibrated so a marginal exit at ~10 sat/vB (~3.1k sats) leaves the holder most of the value, though a *first*-exiter at 10 sat/vB (16k sats) is already underwater on a floor leaf and a 50 sat/vB environment strands floor leaves outright. Value below the floor cannot occupy its own leaf: micropayment residue and sub-floor arkoor change ride **uncheckpointed**, bounded by the 50k-sat aggregate VaR cap, until consolidated into a checkpointable balance, swapped out through an LN gateway, or — for truly abandoned residue — eventually swept by Q at `T_exp + 4032`. This is conceded as open problem #4: value near its exit cost is economically strandable on demand, and the anti-dust floor pushes micropayments into the trust-bounded uncheckpointed tier.

## 4. Mass exit

Per 1,024-leaf pool round (16 cells × radix-4 depth-3), with TRUC packaging done honestly — ephemeral-dust anchors must be spent in the same package and TRUC permits only 1-parent-1-child, so **each zero-fee parent needs its own ≈115 vB CPFP child; batch-bumping by towers or Q is impossible under the design's own relay rules**:

- 336 node txs (16 × 21) ≈ 107.9 kvB
- 1,024 claim txs ≈ 197.6 kvB
- 1,360 CPFP children (336 + 1,024, one per parent) ≈ 156.4 kvB

Total ≈ 462 kvB ⇒ **≈451 vB per user, ≈0.46 blocks per pool round**. 100k users ≈ 98 pool rounds ≈ **46 blocks (~7.7 hours) of full chain saturation** — comparable in shape to Lightning factory mass-exit analyses where forced unfurling is O(log n) txs per user ([Sguanci & Sidiropoulos, arXiv:2208.01908](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.01908)). (v0.2's ~310 vB/user and ~0.33 blocks/pool assumed tower-batched children, which the 1p1c rule forbids.)

What makes this survivable rather than catastrophic:

- **Hourly `T_exp` laddering.** Pools are funded hourly, so expiries never synchronize organically; each pool's deadline sits ~6 blocks from its neighbors'. Only an adversary who forces the timing (sustained multi-operator DoS, [attacks.md](attacks.md)) compresses the schedule — and even then the result is fees, never theft.
- **1008-block margins.** The wallet refresh deadline is `T_exp − 1008` (~1 week) and tower height-deposits fire by `T_exp − 720` (and immediately on conflict detection), so even the 46-block backlog at 100k-user scale has a ~22× time buffer against its own deadline at normal throughput; a single pool round's 0.46 blocks is negligible.
- **Keyless fee-bumping.** All exit packages are pre-signed TRUC with P2A anchors; anyone — towers, operators, altruists — can fund a priced-out user's exit.
- **Consensus ordering.** The claim ladder removes owner-vs-owner mempool races; exits are pre-signed and counterparty-free, so congestion delays them but cannot invalidate them.

**Fee-spike sensitivity.** The per-user mass-exit cost (451 vB) scales linearly with the fee environment: ~4.5k sats at 10 sat/vB, ~22.5k at 50, ~45k at 100. A sustained 50+ sat/vB spike concurrent with a forced mass exit puts floor-denomination leaves outright underwater (§3) and turns the event into a regressive tax on small holders. Replacement cycling against forfeit/exit CPFP packages has no complete mempool-level fix (open problem #3); the defense is margins, dedicated fee UTXOs, and tower redundancy — mitigation, not elimination.

## 5. Sensitivity analysis

The model has four axes: cost of capital, the `sweep_delay` policy, refresh behavior, and the L1 fee environment.

**Cost of capital × refresh fee.** Break-even refresh fee ≈ APY × (4320 + sweep_delay)/52560 — the ~58-day lock as a fraction of the year (§1.2):

| Operator cost of capital | Break-even fee per refresh | Posted 0.5–0.9% per refresh |
|---|---|---|
| 2% APY | 0.32% | comfortable margin |
| 5% APY (modeled) | 0.80% | ≈break-even near the top of the band; margin lives in cosign/gateway/pinning |
| 8% APY | 1.27% | refresh line loses money across the whole band; fees must reprice toward ~1.3% per refresh |
| 12% APY | 1.91% | ≈2.5%/month at the 23-day cycle — uncompetitive with custodial alternatives |

**Sweep delay.** `sweep_delay` sets both the capital lock and the dormancy grace window: lock = `(4320 + sweep_delay)/144` days; grace = `sweep_delay − 288` blocks. Canonical 4032 → 58-day lock, float ≈2.5× hot at the 23-day cycle, break-even 0.80% per refresh at 5% APY, 3,744-block (~26-day) grace. Halving it to 2016 → ~44-day lock, float ≈1.9×, break-even ≈0.60% per refresh — but the grace window collapses to 1,728 blocks (~12 days) for towers to exit dormant funds before the sweep (§2.3). Cheap refreshes and safe dormancy pull on the same parameter; a pool cannot have both ends.

**Refresh cadence** is an explicit axis on both sides of the ledger: the user's monthly cost is fee × 30 d/cycle and Q's float is hot × 58 d/cycle (§1.2 table), so for the operator the two scale together and roughly cancel as long as the fee tracks the per-refresh break-even. What does not cancel is the user-facing price — high-velocity users pay proportionally more, which is why hop-4 merchants sweep through gateways (§2.2) and why the minimum dwell time D bounds hostile churn (§1.1). Higher pool-wide hot fraction likewise raises float and refresh revenue proportionally.

**Fee environment** moves exit costs and cell-tx overhead linearly. The dust floor, VaR cap, and mass-exit math are all calibrated around ~10 sat/vB; a regime shift to sustained 50–100 sat/vB would require raising the leaf floor (worsening the micropayment concession) and widens the strandable-value band.

**Where the model breaks**, explicitly:

1. **High-yield environments.** If operators can earn ≫5% on BTC elsewhere, refresh fees must rise toward 1.3–1.9% per refresh (≈1.7–2.5%/month at the 23-day cycle) and the hot-balance proposition collapses against custodial offerings. The carrying cost is structural to covenant-less Ark capital fronting (open problem #7, whose magnitude roughly doubled once the 58-day lock and dormant float were modeled honestly); no parameter tuning removes it — `sweep_delay` only trades it against dormancy safety.
2. **Sub-scale pools.** Fixed costs (hourly cell txs, fee-UTXO reserves, 7-org infrastructure) don't amortize below a few thousand active users; the cosign margin at 10k users is already thin (§1.3). The business wants 100k+ users or substantial gateway volume.
3. **Sustained fee spikes.** Above ~50 sat/vB the 10k-sat floor leaf is strandable and mass-exit becomes punitive for small holders; above ~100 sat/vB even mid-size leaves are marginal.
4. **Tower market failure.** Sender-funded height-deposits assume a functioning fee-budgeted tower market; if it stays thin (open problem #10), operators must run towers themselves — a cost line and a centralization regression.
5. **Mispriced churn limits.** Per-refresh pricing closes the float-drain lever only with the minimum dwell time D and per-leaf rate limits enforced — leverage is bounded by 58 d / D (≈8× at D = 7 d) against ~1,390× unthrottled (§1.1); loosening D for UX reopens the attack.

## 6. Comparison economics

Full feature comparison in [comparison.md](comparison.md); this section is costs only.

**vs Lightning.** A Lightning self-custody user amortizes: channel open + close (two on-chain txs per channel, repeated per reopen/splice/force-close), purchased inbound liquidity (a recurring cost because received payments deplete it — [lightning.engineering](https://docs.lightning.engineering/the-lightning-network/liquidity/how-to-get-inbound-capacity-on-the-lightning-network)), rebalancing fees, and a fat-tailed force-close cost. Flock replaces all of it with one transparent 0.5–0.9% per refresh on hot balances (≈1%/month at the deadline-driven 23-day cycle) plus ≈451 vB of amortized exit per user (≈310 vB when the path is already unrolled). Compared at the **same feerates**: at 10 sat/vB a Lightning open+close cycle (~700 vB) costs ~7k sats per channel against Flock's ~4.5k amortized exit share; at 50 sat/vB Lightning's pathological force-close with full HTLC slots can exceed 1M sats ([voltage.cloud](https://voltage.cloud/blog/lightning-node-management-handling-high-on-chain-fees)) against Flock's ~80k worst-case first exit and ~22.5k amortized. The scope of the win is stated in §2.1: competitive with Lightning's open/close/rebalance amortization for active payment balances — at ~0.8% per refresh (≈1%/month hot), a large long-held balance belongs in a channel or in Flock dormancy, not hot. Lightning retains the edge for high-volume routing nodes whose channels amortize over enormous flow, for payee finality, and for privacy ([comparison.md](comparison.md)).

**vs single-operator Ark.** Identical cost *structure* — the float, the refresh pass-through, the exit tree — because Flock is covenant-less Ark. Three economic differences: (1) idle funds: Bark-class designs sweep unrefreshed coins at expiry ([bitcoinops.org](https://bitcoinops.org/en/topics/ark/)) — keep paying refresh fees or face confiscation; Flock's dormant funds pay no carrying fee and are never confiscated while towers function, but are automatically exited on-chain ~32 days after the last checkpoint at the owner's expense (≈5–18k sats incl. the contingent tower fee, §2.3), with quorum confiscation only if the owner and every tower fail to act for ~26 days after recovery maturity. (2) Lifetime-priced refresh ([second.tech](https://second.tech/docs/learn/intro)) carries the churn-amplification exposure that per-refresh duration pricing plus the minimum dwell time D closes (§1.1). (3) The federation halves nothing financially — same float per pool — but converts operator failure from "exit now or lose funds" into a 3-of-7 outage tolerance, which is worth real money in avoided panic exits.

**vs Cashu/Fedimint.** Custody is free: zero on-chain footprint, zero carrying cost, blind-signature privacy ([NIP-60/61](https://nips.nostr.com/60)). Flock cannot beat free and does not try; the entire fee schedule is the price of removing the mint's ability to rug. Only designs with cheap dormancy compete with Cashu's hold-for-free property at all ([research/raw/](../research/raw/) review artifacts) — which is why Flock has dormancy, and why this document prices it honestly: no carrying fee, but one automatic ~5–18k-sat on-chain exit ~32 days after the last checkpoint, tower dependence, and a ~26-day worst-case window (§2.3) — protected, not free.

**vs Spark.** Statechain transfers are nearly costless off-chain and need no per-payment operator capital ([docs.spark.money](https://docs.spark.money/learn/transfers)), undercutting Flock's cosign+refresh fees — but the key-deletion assumption is unprovable, so the lower fee buys a permanently unfalsifiable trust assumption rather than Flock's time-bounded, checkpoint-terminated one ([comparison.md](comparison.md)).
